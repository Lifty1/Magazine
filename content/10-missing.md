## The huia

**Last confirmed: 28 December 1907, Tararua Range**

The huia lived only in the forests of the southern North Island, and nothing else looked like it. Both sexes were glossy black with orange wattles and a white band across the tip of the tail, but the male had a short, stout, chisel-like bill and the female a long, slender, curved one, the most extreme difference between the sexes in bill shape of any known bird. The male hacked into rotten wood; the female probed the holes. They were said to be inseparable.

The huia was tapu, and its tail feathers were among the most precious things a Māori chief could wear, traded the length of the country. Then Europeans wanted them too. Specimens were shot for museums and collectors; pairs were shot together. In 1901 the visiting Duke of York, later George V, was given a huia feather for his hatband at Rotorua, and the fashion took off. Rats, stoats and forest clearance did the rest.

On 28 December 1907 the naturalist W. W. Smith saw three huia in the Tararua Range. It is the last sighting anyone has accepted. There were credible reports into the 1920s, and a few after; nobody followed them up in time. In 1909 a search party went into the Ruahine Range looking for the bird and came back with nothing. One of its guides, Henare Hāmana, could imitate the huia's call. Around 1949 he was taken into a Wellington studio and recorded whistling it. It is the only huia call that exists.

In 2024 a single huia tail feather sold at auction in Auckland for $46,521.50, the most ever paid for a feather.

## The South Island kōkako

**Last accepted: 2007, near Reefton**

The kōkako is a slate-grey forest bird with a black mask and a song often described as the most beautiful in New Zealand: slow, rich, organ-like phrases sung in duet. The North Island species survives, rescued by predator control. The South Island species, with orange wattles where the northern bird has blue, has been on the edge of existence for most of a century.

Its record is a perfect illustration of how hard it is to declare something gone. The last authenticated sighting, for a long time, was at Mount Aspiring in 1967, and in 2007, forty years later, the Department of Conservation declared the species extinct. In 2013 the Ornithological Society accepted a sighting made by two people near Reefton in 2007, and the bird was moved from *extinct* to *data deficient*. The Department's own report was ambivalent: probably "functionally extinct", but "we are not convinced beyond reasonable doubt that the last individual of these taxa have died".

Reports keep coming: from the Heaphy Track, from the Old Ghost Road, from the bush near Tuatapere, where a hunter heard a strange call and saw a large grey bird on New Year's Day 2024. A feather found in 1995 and long argued over was retested in 2025 and confirmed, again, to belong to a blackbird. The South Island Kōkako Trust offers a reward of $10,000 for evidence a panel of ornithologists will accept, and it is clear about what that means: a photograph or a feather, not a story.

## The thylacine

**Last known: 7 September 1936, Hobart**

The thylacine, or Tasmanian tiger, was the largest carnivorous marsupial of modern times: a striped, dog-shaped animal with a stiff tail and jaws that opened astonishingly wide. It was gone from mainland Australia thousands of years ago, and in Tasmania it was hunted down under government and private bounties as a supposed killer of sheep. The last known thylacine died at the Beaumaris Zoo in Hobart on 7 September 1936. In 1933 the naturalist David Fleay had filmed it in its cage for about forty-five seconds, and was bitten on the backside for his trouble. That grainy footage is the image most people carry of the species.

Under the rules of the time an animal could not be declared extinct until fifty years had passed without a confirmed record, and so the thylacine was declared extinct by the IUCN in 1982 and by the Tasmanian government in 1986. Sightings have never stopped. Neither have searches: camera traps, footprint casts, a two-year confidential hunt in the 1980s after a park ranger, Hans Naarding, reported a thylacine in his spotlight. Nothing confirmed. Australia marks National Threatened Species Day every 7 September.

## The ivory-billed woodpecker

**Last widely accepted: 1944, Louisiana**

The ivory-bill was the giant of North American woodpeckers, black and white with a bone-coloured beak and, in the male, a flaming red crest, and it lived in the great old swamp forests of the American South until they were cut down. The last universally accepted sightings were in the 1940s. Then, in 2004, a kayaker reported one in the Big Woods of Arkansas, and the next year a team of scientists announced in the journal *Science* that the bird had been rediscovered, on the strength of a few seconds of blurry video. Thousands of people went looking. Nobody got a clear picture.

In 2021 the US Fish and Wildlife Service proposed taking the ivory-bill off the endangered list because it was extinct. The public response was so divided that the agency extended its deadline in 2022 and, in 2023, deferred the decision with no new date. At the last formal word, the ivory-billed woodpecker was neither officially alive nor officially extinct. It may be the only bird in the world whose status is, technically, pending.

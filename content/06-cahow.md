Spanish sailors in the sixteenth century regarded Bermuda with suspicion. The reefs were bad enough, but the islands were also loud. At night, the story goes, the air above them was full of wailing and moaning, and they became known as the Isle of Devils. Bermudians will tell you the devils were birds: a gadfly petrel the size of a pigeon that nested in burrows on the islands in huge numbers and called to its mate in the dark with a cry that sounded, to English ears, like *cahow*.

The Spaniards did learn to stop and eat them. In 1603 one Spanish captain, Diego Ramírez, wrote that his men could take up to four thousand birds in a night. The hogs they released on the islands to provide fresh meat for later voyages rooted the birds' burrows out of the ground.

Then, in 1609, the English ship *Sea Venture*, bound for Virginia, was wrecked on the reef. It is the wreck that is generally thought to have given Shakespeare *The Tempest*. Most of the survivors eventually sailed on to Jamestown in two boats they built from the wreckage, but Bermuda's permanent English settlement dates from them, and the settlers ate cahows too, the fattest birds and the eggs, especially in the lean winter months when the birds came ashore to breed. They brought rats, cats and dogs, and burned the forest.

In 1616 the governor issued a proclamation "against the spoyle and havocke of the Cohowes", one of the earliest conservation laws in the Americas. It made no difference. By the 1620s the cahow was gone. For the next three centuries it was a word in old documents, and any seabird reported off Bermuda that might have been one was assumed to be something else.

## The bird in the lighthouse

In June 1935 an unfamiliar seabird flew into the lighthouse at St David's, at the eastern end of Bermuda, and was killed. The naturalist William Beebe, who was working on the islands, sent it to Robert Cushman Murphy, a seabird specialist at the American Museum of Natural History in New York. Murphy identified it as a cahow. It was a single dead bird, and it could have come from anywhere. But if it had come from Bermuda, the cahow would have to be breeding somewhere nearby.

Murphy came to look for himself in 1951, with Louis Mowbray of the Bermuda Government Aquarium. They brought along a local boy who was mad about birds: David Wingate, fifteen years old. On 28 January 1951, on a cluster of tiny rocky islets in Castle Harbour, a sheltered bay at Bermuda's eastern end, they found cahows nesting in deep crevices in the rock. There were eighteen pairs. They were probably the whole species.

## The boy who stayed

Most people who are present at a rediscovery go back to their lives. Wingate didn't. He went to Cornell University to study zoology and came home in 1958 to take on the cahow, and in 1966 he became Bermuda's first government conservation officer, a job he held until 2000.

The problems he faced were the kind that make conservationists go grey. The nesting islets were tiny, about a hectare between them, and short of soil for burrows. Rats swam out to them. White-tailed tropicbirds, a native species bigger and more aggressive than the cahow, were evicting cahows from the few good crevices and sometimes killing their chicks. A cahow pair raises at most one chick a year, and a young bird spends years at sea before returning to breed, so the population could not bounce back from anything.

Wingate's answers were practical and patient. He poisoned the rats. He fitted wooden baffles over burrow entrances, cut so that a slim cahow could get in but a tropicbird could not. He built artificial burrows out of concrete. And, because the islets were simply too small, he began restoring Nonsuch Island next door, sixteen acres that centuries of misuse had turned into something close to desert. He replanted it with thousands of Bermuda cedars, palmettos and other native plants, so that the island might one day look as it had before people arrived, and the cahow might have somewhere to go.

## Moving house

The idea was taken up by Wingate's successor, Jeremy Madeiros, who identified the biggest remaining threat as the islets themselves: low, eroding, and exposed to hurricanes and rising seas. In 2003 Hurricane Fabian destroyed about fifteen burrows outright and damaged most of the rest.

Seabirds tend to come back to breed where they fledged, so Madeiros, working with the Australian petrel specialist Nicholas Carlile, began moving chicks to artificial burrows on Nonsuch in 2004, about three weeks before they were due to leave for sea, and hand-feeding them squid and anchovies until they flew. Over five years 102 chicks fledged from Nonsuch. If Carlile's name is familiar, it should be: three years earlier, he had been one of the two scientists who climbed Ball's Pyramid and found the tree lobsters. The world of people who find lost things is a small one.

In February 2008 the first of the translocated birds came back to Nonsuch from the open Atlantic. In January 2009 one laid an egg there, the first cahow egg on Nonsuch for more than three hundred years.

## Counting

In 1960 there were seventeen or eighteen breeding pairs, raising seven or eight chicks a year. In 2019 there were 132 pairs, fledging 72 chicks. In 2023 there were 164 pairs on six islands. Most of them nest in artificial burrows, and a camera in one of them streams the lives of a cahow pair to anyone who wants to watch. The cahow is Bermuda's national bird, and the noise over the islands at night in winter, the noise that once frightened Spanish sailors, is gradually coming back.

kicker: Coda
title: The Whistle
---

In 1909 a party went into the Ruahine Range, in the southern North Island, to look for huia. The bird had not been confirmed alive for two years, but there were rumours, and a hope of finding birds that could still be protected. One of the party's guides was Henare Hāmana, who also went by the name Harry Salmon. He knew the huia's calls, and could whistle them. Māori hunters had long drawn huia close by imitating them.

They found nothing.

Forty years later, in about 1949, a Moawhango farmer and amateur historian named Robert Batley took Hāmana to a recording studio in Wellington. Batley spoke a short introduction, describing a pair of huia feeding on a rotten tree. Then Hāmana whistled: a call, and an answer. The male, and then the female.

It is the only recording of a huia that exists. It is not a recording of a huia. It is a recording of a man remembering one, forty years after the last of them had gone quiet, imitating a sound he had once used to bring them close. You can hear it now, online. It is very short.

Every story in this magazine is about the hope that something we thought was gone might still be out there. It is a good hope, and sometimes it comes true. But the whistle is the other half of the truth. Most of the time, when something goes, what is left is a memory in one person, and then a recording of that memory, and then nothing at all.

The takahē, the kākāpō, the tāiko and the storm petrel are still here, and so are the people who spend their lives keeping them. Go and hear them while they can still answer.

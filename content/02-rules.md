kicker: The rules
title: How to Declare Something Extinct
---

It is surprisingly hard to prove that something no longer exists. You cannot photograph an absence. So conservation bodies have had to decide what counts as enough.

**The old rule was time.** For much of the twentieth century a species was not declared extinct until fifty years had passed without a confirmed record. That is why the thylacine, whose last known individual died in Hobart in 1936, was not formally declared extinct until 1982.

**The modern rule is doubt.** Today the IUCN Red List, the global authority, calls a species extinct when "there is no reasonable doubt that the last individual has died". To get there, exhaustive surveys have to fail to find it in its known and expected habitat, at the right times of day and year, across its historic range, over a period that makes sense for the animal's life cycle. A bird that lives for decades needs a longer search than a beetle that lives a season.

**In between, there is a holding pen.** Species that are probably gone, but not certainly, can be listed as *Critically Endangered (Possibly Extinct)*. New Zealand's threat classification system has a category, *Data Deficient*, that can do a similar job.

**The rules change, and so do the answers.** In 2007 the Department of Conservation declared the South Island kōkako extinct, forty years after the last accepted sighting. In 2012 it brought its criteria into line with the international standard of reasonable doubt; in 2013 a 2007 sighting near Reefton was accepted, and the bird went from *extinct* to *data deficient* without any new confirmed sighting at all.

**Both mistakes are costly.** Declare a species extinct too early, and the money and protection stop while a few individuals may still be clinging on. Ornithologists call this a *Romeo error*, after Shakespeare's hero, who gave up on Juliet because she looked dead. Keep a species on the books too long, and scarce conservation money goes on searching for ghosts instead of saving the living.

**Sometimes nobody can decide.** In 2021 the United States government proposed declaring the ivory-billed woodpecker extinct. In 2023, faced with deep disagreement among experts, it put the decision off indefinitely.

Most of the species in this magazine have fallen, at one time or another, into one of these gaps.

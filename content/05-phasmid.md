Lord Howe Island, a crescent of forest and reef about 600 kilometres off the coast of New South Wales, used to have an insect so common that people fished with it. Large, black, wingless stick insects, as long as a hand and heavy as a small bird, lived in the hollows of its trees and came out at night to eat the leaves. The islanders called them tree lobsters and used them for bait.

In 1918 the supply ship *Makambo* ran aground on the island. Black rats came ashore. Within a couple of years there were no tree lobsters left. After 1920 nobody could find one, and *Dryococelus australis*, the Lord Howe Island stick insect, was added to the long list of island species that had met a rat and lost.

Twenty kilometres to the south-east of Lord Howe stands Ball's Pyramid: the eroded plug of an old volcano, more than 550 metres of bare rock rising sheer out of rough water, only 300 metres across at its widest. It is the tallest sea stack in the world. Nobody lives there. There is almost nowhere to land. It was named for Lieutenant Henry Lidgbird Ball of the Royal Navy, who sighted it in 1788, and for most of the next two centuries it was looked at rather than visited.

## Droppings

In 1964 a team of climbers from Sydney made an attempt on the summit and found, on the rock, the body of a large stick insect. Over the following years other climbers came across more fresh carcasses. Something seemed to be living out there. But expeditions to find live insects came back empty-handed.

In February 2001 two Australian scientists, David Priddel and Nicholas Carlile, went out to Ball's Pyramid with two companions to settle the question. They had done the arithmetic: there seemed to be just enough vegetation on the stack to support a small population. They climbed about 120 metres up a steep grassy slope and found nothing but crickets. On the way down, in a crevice roughly 100 metres above the sea, they came to a single shrub, a *Melaleuca*, growing out of the rock. Underneath it were droppings. Large ones.

Stick insects of this kind feed at night. So Carlile came back after dark with a local ranger, Dean Hiscox, a camera and torches, and scrambled up the slope again. Under that one bush, in the leaf litter that had built up around its roots, they counted twenty-four Lord Howe Island stick insects. As far as anyone could tell, they were the entire wild population of the species: two dozen animals, under one bush, on the side of a spike of rock in the middle of the sea.

## Adam and Eve

The authorities waited two years before taking any. A species that small can be wiped out by a single rockfall or a single dry season, but it can also be wiped out by people taking too many to save it. In 2003 a team from the New South Wales national parks service went back and collected two breeding pairs. One pair went to a private breeder in Sydney. The other, quickly nicknamed Adam and Eve, went to Melbourne Zoo.

There were difficulties at first. Then the insects started to breed, and kept on. They turned out to have a remarkable range of talents for a species on the brink. The young hatch bright green and active by day, and darken to a glossy black as they grow and switch to the night shift. Males and females form something like pair bonds, sleeping side by side with the male's legs draped over the female. And females can, if no males are available, produce young without them, which may be exactly how a handful of insects survived eighty years on a rock.

By 2008, when Jane Goodall visited, Melbourne Zoo had 700 insects and more than 11,000 eggs. By 2016 more than 13,000 eggs had hatched there, and eggs had been sent to Bristol, San Diego and Toronto to found insurance colonies. Budapest Zoo became the first outside Australia to breed them, in 2012; Prague joined the programme in 2025. A 2017 genetic study compared insects from Ball's Pyramid with museum specimens collected on Lord Howe a century earlier and confirmed what everyone had hoped: they were the same species, not a lookalike cousin.

There were surprises on the rock, too. In 2014 a team of climbers, there without permission, photographed live stick insects near the summit, around 500 metres up, in sedge growing from very thin soil. The population had never been as confined to its one shrub as it looked. It had simply been found in the one place anyone had searched.

## Going home

The hard part, as ever, is not breeding the animal but undoing the reason it vanished. In 2019 Lord Howe Island carried out one of the most ambitious rodent eradications ever attempted on an inhabited island. Then, in April 2021, a rat turned up. A small remnant had evaded the poison; it was tracked down, the monitoring was changed, and the work goes on. Plans have been drawn up to test a return on Blackburn Island, an islet in the Lord Howe lagoon, before any release on the main island.

So, at the time of writing, the tree lobsters have not yet gone home. Thousands of them are living in zoos on three continents, descended from a handful of insects taken from under one bush. Somewhere on a spike of rock in the Tasman Sea, their wild relatives are still doing what they have done since 1920: eating, sleeping in pairs, and waiting for the rats to leave.

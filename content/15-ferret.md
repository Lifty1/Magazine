standfirst: The black-footed ferret was declared extinct in 1979. Two years later a ranch dog called Shep killed one. What happened next, all the way to a clone born in 2020, shows what de-extinction technology is actually good for.

The black-footed ferret is a slim, sand-coloured weasel with a black mask and black feet, and it lives almost entirely on prairie dogs. It sleeps in their burrows, hunts them at night in their burrows, and raises its kits in their burrows. Up to nine-tenths of its diet is prairie dog. So when the prairie dogs of the American West were poisoned by the million to clear land for cattle, and then struck by sylvatic plague, an introduced disease, the ferret went with them.

By 1974 the last known wild population, in South Dakota, had vanished. A few had been taken into captivity, but they bred badly, and by the end of the decade the last of them had died. In 1979 the species was declared extinct.

On 26 September 1981, on a ranch outside the small town of Meeteetse, in north-western Wyoming, John and Lucille Hogg found that their dog, Shep, had killed an animal they didn't recognise. Lucille took it to a local taxidermist. The taxidermist knew exactly what it was, and called the wildlife authorities.

A month later, biologists searching the prairie dog colonies around Meeteetse at night with spotlights found a living black-footed ferret, and then others. The extinct species had a population.

## The second loss

It is the takahē story again, down to the dog. And like the takahē, the ferret immediately showed that finding a species and saving it are two different problems.

In 1985 plague struck the Meeteetse prairie dogs, and canine distemper, which is lethal to ferrets, broke out among the ferrets themselves. The population collapsed. Between 1985 and 1987 every remaining wild ferret that could be found was caught and taken into captivity, a decision that was bitterly controversial at the time. The last was trapped in February 1987. There were eighteen of them: seven males and eleven females. Every black-footed ferret alive today is descended from seven of those animals that went on to breed.

The captive breeding worked. The first kits were born that same year at a research centre in Wyoming. In 1991 forty-nine ferrets were released into the Shirley Basin, in south-eastern Wyoming. Since then ferrets have been returned to prairie grasslands in eight US states, in Canada and in Mexico, and more than ten thousand kits have been born in human care.

## Willa

The problem with descending from seven animals is that you are all cousins. Inbreeding leaves a species prone to disease and poor fertility, and there is no way to add new genes to a population that has none left to add.

Except, it turned out, from the freezer. In 1988 a female from the last wild group, named Willa, died, and cells from her body were frozen and stored. She had left no living descendants, so her genes were missing from the captive population. On 10 December 2020 a clone of Willa, named Elizabeth Ann, was born to a domestic ferret surrogate: the first clone of an endangered species native to the United States. Further clones followed, and in late 2024 one of them, Antonia, produced kits of her own. Willa's genes, absent for more than thirty years, were back in the breeding population.

Set this beside the moa. The same family of techniques that Colossal hopes will make a moa-like bird is already being used, quietly, for something more modest and more obviously useful: putting lost diversity back into a living species that is struggling. Nobody is claiming Elizabeth Ann is a resurrection. She is a ferret, born into a species that still exists, because a ranch dog in Wyoming, forty-odd years ago, brought the right animal home.

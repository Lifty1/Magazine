Marjorie Courtenay-Latimer was thirty-one years old, unmarried, untrained, and in charge of a small museum in East London, on the Eastern Cape coast of South Africa. She had been hired in 1931, at twenty-four, after impressing the interview panel with how much she knew about South African natural history. She had no degree. What she had was a working relationship with the local fishing fleet, whose crews she had asked to put aside anything strange that came up in their nets.

On 22 December 1938 the trawler *Nerine* came in under Captain Hendrik Goosen, and someone telephoned to say there were specimens on board. She went down to have a look, and to wish the crew a happy Christmas. Among the pile of sharks and rays on the deck was something blue.

"I picked away at the layers of slime to reveal the most beautiful fish I had ever seen," she wrote later. "It was five feet long, a pale mauvy blue with faint flecks of whitish spots; it had an iridescent silver-blue-green sheen all over. It was covered in hard scales, and it had four limb-like fins and a strange puppy-dog tail." It looked, she thought, like a big china ornament. She had no idea what it was.

## A fish in a taxi

What followed would be farce if the stakes had been lower. She and her assistant got the fish, five feet long and heavy, into a taxi and took it back to the museum. She couldn't find it in any of her books. The museum had no cold storage, so she tried the town mortuary, which refused to take a fish, and then a cold store, which also refused. In the end she went to a taxidermist she knew, Robert Center, who helped her wrap it in newspaper and sheets soaked in formalin.

She wrote to the one person she thought might be able to identify it: J. L. B. Smith, a chemistry lecturer at Rhodes University in Grahamstown who was also a self-taught and obsessive ichthyologist. She sent a description and a rough sketch. But it was Christmas, and Smith was away on holiday. By 27 December the fish was rotting in the summer heat and leaking oil, and she made the decision that would haunt the story: she let Center skin it and gut it so that at least the skin could be mounted. The soft parts went in the rubbish.

When Smith finally saw her sketch, he recognised it immediately and could hardly believe what he was recognising. He cabled back: MOST IMPORTANT PRESERVE SKELETON AND GILLS = FISH DESCRIBED. It was too late for the gills. On 16 February 1939 he arrived in East London and stood in front of the mounted skin. "There was not a shadow of a doubt," he said. "It could have been one of those creatures of 200 million years ago come alive again."

It was a coelacanth, a member of a group of lobe-finned fishes that turns up in rocks more than 400 million years old and was thought to have vanished at the end of the Cretaceous, 66 million years ago, along with the dinosaurs. Lobe-finned fishes are more closely related to us than they are to a trout or a snapper: their stalked, fleshy fins are built on the same plan as the limbs of the first animals to walk on land. The coelacanth was a textbook fossil, a branch of the tree everyone assumed had been sawn off. Smith named it *Latimeria chalumnae*, after the woman who had saved it and the river off whose mouth it was caught.

## Fourteen years of leaflets

A single stuffed skin, without its organs, is a frustrating thing to have discovered. Smith was certain the 1938 fish had strayed from somewhere else, and he spent the next fourteen years trying to find out where. He printed leaflets in English, Portuguese and French, with a photograph of the fish and an offer of £100 for a specimen, and had them distributed in ports up and down the east coast of Africa. "Look carefully at this fish," they began.

It took until December 1952. A fisherman off Anjouan, in the Comoro Islands between Mozambique and Madagascar, brought a five-foot fish to a British trader and sea captain named Eric Hunt, who had one of Smith's leaflets and knew what he was looking at. He telegraphed Smith. Smith, terrified that the specimen would rot or be claimed by the French authorities who governed the islands, did something only a man in his state of mind would try: he tracked down the Prime Minister of South Africa, D. F. Malan, over Christmas, and talked him into lending an air force Dakota to fly to the Comoros and fetch it.

The new fish was missing one of its two dorsal fins, and Smith, convinced it was something new, named it *Malania anjouanae*, after his prime minister. It was later shown to be the same species as the first, with a deformity. The French, embarrassed and furious, closed the islands to foreign coelacanth hunters.

The deeper embarrassment was that the fish had not really been lost. Comorian fishermen, hand-lining at night from canoes over the steep volcanic slopes, had been catching the occasional *gombessa* for a long time. It was not prized; the flesh is oily. Science had written off an animal that the people who lived above it knew perfectly well.

## The second species

Between 1938 and 1975 some 84 coelacanths were caught and recorded, almost all around the Comoros. Since then they have been found off Kenya, Tanzania, Mozambique and Madagascar, and, in 2000, by divers in the canyons off Sodwana Bay in South Africa's iSimangaliso Wetland Park, further up the same coast the *Nerine* had trawled.

Then, in September 1997, a marine biologist named Mark Erdmann and his wife, Arnaz Mehta, were walking through a fish market in Manado, on the Indonesian island of Sulawesi, and saw a coelacanth for sale. It was brown, not blue. They took a few photographs before it was sold. Erdmann went back to Sulawesi, talked to fishermen, and in July 1998 a fisherman brought him a second one. It was a new species, nearly nine thousand kilometres from the Comoros: *Latimeria menadoensis*, known locally as *raja laut*, king of the sea. Genetic studies suggest the two living species parted company tens of millions of years ago.

## Slow life

What we have learned since makes the coelacanth stranger, not less strange. They live in caves and on rocky slopes, typically a couple of hundred metres down, drifting almost motionless at night as they hunt, sometimes hanging head-down or swimming backwards. Females give birth to live young. A 2021 study that read growth rings in their scales concluded that coelacanths may live for around a century, do not mature until they are roughly fifty-five, and carry their young for about five years, longer than any other animal's known pregnancy.

A fish that lives that slowly cannot absorb much loss. The West Indian Ocean coelacanth is listed as Critically Endangered, mostly because of deep-water fishing nets that catch it by accident. The Indonesian species is listed as Vulnerable.

## What the curator knew

Courtenay-Latimer spent the rest of her working life at the East London Museum. The skin she saved is still there, the holotype of its species, the fish against which every other coelacanth is measured. She died in 2004, aged 97.

It is tempting to tell the coelacanth story as the triumph of the professor: Smith, with his cables and leaflets and borrowed aeroplane. But the fish was found because a woman with no qualifications had persuaded a trawler crew to keep the odd things they caught, and then fought a mortuary, a cold store and a South African summer to keep it from rotting. Almost every rediscovery in this magazine began the same way: with someone who was paying attention to what other people threw back.

title: The Tree in the Canyon
standfirst: A parks officer, out exploring canyons with friends, walked past a tree that wasn't supposed to exist.
---

David Noble was a field officer with the New South Wales parks service, and in his spare time he went canyoning: abseiling into the narrow, wet gorges that cut through the sandstone of the Wollemi wilderness, north-west of Sydney, looking for ones nobody had been down before. In September 1994, with two friends, Michael Casteleyn and Tony Zimmerman, he dropped into a gorge so deep and sheltered that it held a patch of rainforest at the bottom. Among the trees was one he didn't recognise.

It was tall, with fern-like foliage and some of the strangest bark he had ever seen: dark brown, knobbly, bubbled all over. People have since compared it, not entirely unkindly, to a bowl of Coco Pops. Noble knew enough botany to know that he didn't know what it was, and he took a sample.

Botanists at the parks service and the Royal Botanic Gardens in Sydney worked it out. It was a new genus in the Araucariaceae, the ancient family of conifers that includes the monkey puzzle, the Norfolk Island pine and, most closely of all, New Zealand's kauri. It belonged to a lineage that had otherwise been known, if at all, only as fossils. It was named *Wollemia nobilis*: for the wilderness, and for the man.

## A family of one

It is the only species in its genus, and there are fewer than sixty adult trees in the wild, in a few stands not far apart. Many of them have multiple trunks, some of them dozens, sprouting from the same base, which makes the trees hard to count and suggests they are very old. Every wild Wollemi pine that has been tested is genetically almost indistinguishable from every other. At some point, thousands of years ago, the species passed through a bottleneck so narrow that its whole surviving population is, to all intents, one family.

That makes it extraordinarily vulnerable, and the location of the grove has been kept secret ever since. It has not been secret enough. In 2005 some of the wild trees were found to be infected with *Phytophthora cinnamomi*, a root-rotting water mould, which park rangers believe was carried in by unauthorised visitors.

Then came fire. In the summer of 2019–20, when bushfires burned across south-eastern Australia, the blaze reached the Wollemi wilderness. Specialist firefighters were winched down into the gorge to set up an irrigation system around the trees, and aircraft dropped fire retardant around them. The fire came through. The grove survived.

## Hiding in plain sight

The strategy that may save the Wollemi pine in the long run is the opposite of secrecy. Botanists found early on that the tree could be grown from cuttings, and botanic gardens around the world now hold them. From the mid-2000s they were sold to the public, and Wollemi pines are now planted in parks and gardens, and grown in pots as Christmas trees. The idea is simple: if the tree is everywhere, people have less reason to go looking for the original grove, and the species no longer depends on one gorge.

It is an odd fate for a tree whose lineage outlived the dinosaurs. The last of its line, found by accident at the bottom of a canyon in 1994, now sits on patios.

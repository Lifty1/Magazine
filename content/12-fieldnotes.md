## Dirt

**De Winton's golden mole**, last seen 1937, found 2023

A blind, iridescent mole that "swims" through the sand dunes of South Africa's west coast, leaving almost no trace on the surface. Nobody had recorded one for 86 years. In 2021 a team from the Endangered Wildlife Trust and the University of Pretoria walked the dunes collecting soil, and tested it for environmental DNA: the traces of skin and hair that every animal sheds into its surroundings. They also brought a sniffer dog trained to recognise golden moles. The DNA turned up in the soil from Port Nolloth to Lambert's Bay. The mole was announced as rediscovered in 2023, and may be far more widespread than anyone had guessed.

## Cameras

**Attenborough's long-beaked echidna**, collected 1961, filmed 2023

A spiny, egg-laying mammal known to science from a single specimen collected in the Cyclops Mountains of New Guinea in 1961, named after Sir David Attenborough. In 2023 an expedition led by Oxford scientists, working with local Papuan partners, left trail cameras in the forest. When the memory cards were checked, one held the first footage of the animal alive.

**Silver-backed chevrotain**, last recorded 1990, photographed 2019

A deer-like animal the size of a rabbit, unrecorded by science for nearly thirty years. Local rangers and villagers in southern Vietnam said they still saw it. Camera traps set where they pointed proved them right.

## People who know

**Wallace's giant bee**, rediscovered 1981, then lost again; filmed 2019

The largest bee in the world, a female with a wingspan of around six centimetres and jaws like a stag beetle's, living inside the nests of tree-dwelling termites in Indonesia's North Moluccas. In 2019 a single female was found, nesting in a termite colony in a tree on Halmahera, by the local naturalist Iswan Maujud, part of an expedition co-led by Clay Bolt and Eli Wyman.

**Fernandina Island Galápagos tortoise**, last seen 1906, found 2019

Known from one male shot by a museum expedition in 1906, and from droppings and bite marks found over the following century. In February 2019 an expedition of park rangers and scientists found an elderly female. In 2021 genetic tests confirmed she belonged to the lost lineage.

## Lists

In 2017 the conservation group now called Re:wild published a list of the 25 "most wanted" lost species: animals and plants not recorded for at least a decade. Several on that list, including the golden mole, the echidna, the chevrotain and the giant bee, have since been found, and others have rotated in to replace them. A sister project, the Search for Lost Birds, launched a list in 2022 of about 160 bird species. By April 2026 it was down to 120. Five were re-found in 2025 alone, almost all of them photographed by birders and guides, local and visiting, who simply went and looked.

## The lesson

Almost none of these animals were rediscovered by accident. They were found because someone decided they might still be there, chose a place, and went. Many were found because local people had never stopped seeing them, and someone finally listened. Lost, more often than we admit, means unlooked-for.

Some time around 1849, a party of sealers working Dusky Sound, in the wet south-western corner of the South Island, set their dogs on a bird they did not recognise. It was big, round and flightless, blue and green with a heavy red beak, and it ran, by their account, "with great speed". When the dogs cornered it, it screamed and fought. The men kept it alive on board their schooner for three or four days. Then they killed it, roasted it, and ate it, each of them, according to the report that later reached London, "partaking of the dainty, which was declared to be delicious".

The skin was saved by Walter Mantell, a young collector who happened to meet the sealers. He sent it home to his father, Gideon Mantell, the English palaeontologist who had discovered *Iguanodon*, and Gideon knew at once what it was. Two years earlier Walter had sent bird bones from South Taranaki to the anatomist Richard Owen, who gave the creature a name, *Notornis*, the "southern bird", and assumed, like everyone else, that it had gone the way of the moa. Now one had turned up as somebody's dinner. In 1850 Gideon presented the skin to the Zoological Society of London.

That was the takahē's first resurrection, and it set the pattern for the next hundred years. Europeans kept deciding the bird was gone. The bird kept being caught by dogs.

Māori had never thought it was gone. They knew it as *takahē*, from *takahi*, to stamp or trample, and travelled long distances to hunt it. A second bird was taken by Māori on Secretary Island, in Fiordland, in 1851. Then nothing for twenty-eight years, until a rabbiter's dog caught one on the eastern side of Lake Te Anau in 1879. That bird was sold to the museum in Dresden for £105 and was destroyed when the city was bombed in the Second World War.

On 7 August 1898 another dog, called Rough, belonging to a musterer named Jack Ross, caught a female on the shore of the same lake. Ross tried to revive her. She died, and he took her to the Otago Museum, and the government bought her for £250. For many years she was the only mounted takahē in New Zealand, and the only one on display anywhere in the world.

After Rough, the takahē passed into the category of things people claim to have seen. Hunters and trampers kept describing big blue-green birds in the Fiordland bush; one party chased something "the size of a goose, with blue-green feathers and the speed of a racehorse" and failed to catch it. None of it could be proved, and science, quite reasonably, filed the bird under *extinct*.

## The doctor's theory

Geoffrey Orbell grew up on a farm at Pukeuri, near Oamaru, and had been reading about the takahē since he was a boy. He became an eye, ear, nose and throat specialist in Invercargill, a founder of the New Zealand Deerstalkers' Association, and the kind of man who spent his weekends in steep country with a rifle. He also had a theory. If the takahē was anywhere, he thought, it was in the Murchison Mountains, the block of tussock-topped ranges between the western arms of Lake Te Anau, not far from where Rough had made his catch.

In April 1948 Orbell went into the Murchisons with two friends, Rex Watson and Neil McCrostie, on what was nominally a deer-hunting trip. He heard calls he didn't recognise. He found tracks that convinced him the bird was alive.

In November they went back, this time with Joan Telfer. On 20 November 1948, following another set of unfamiliar footprints toward another unfamiliar call, Orbell walked into a remote valley and found three takahē. Two were caught, photographed and released. The photographs went around the world.

The party described the bird's warning call as sounding like someone "whistling to them over a .303 cartridge case", a deerstalker's simile if ever there was one. There is a lake in that valley now, and it is called Lake Orbell.

> Finding the bird was the easy part. What came next took fifty years, a fleet of helicopters, and a great many glove puppets.

## What they found

A takahē is not an elegant bird. It is a rail, a cousin of the pūkeko, but built like a squat, heavy hen: around half a metre tall, about 63 centimetres long, and weighing two and a half kilograms or more. What it lacks in grace it makes up in colour. The head, neck and breast are a deep, silky navy; the wings are peacock blue; the back shades through teal and green to olive at the tail, which is white underneath. The beak is enormous, carmine marbled with red, and runs up into a bright scarlet shield on the forehead. The legs are red too. The wings are small and useless for flying, though a takahē will sometimes flap them to help itself up a slope.

It lives on grass. In summer it stays up in the alpine tussock, plucking a stalk, holding it in one foot like a man with a stick of celery, eating the soft lower part and dropping the rest. When the snow comes, it walks down into the forest and scrub. Pairs stay together for years, probably for life, and defend their territory; they build a bulky nest under the bushes and lay one to three buff-coloured eggs.

It once had a relative in the North Island, a taller, slimmer bird that Māori called moho. The moho is known only from bones.

## The long middle

What happened next is the half of the story that usually gets left out, and it is the more useful half.

The government moved quickly to close off the valley and the country around it. Then it stalled. Some people, including the Forest and Bird Society, argued that the takahē should be left alone to work out its own destiny. Others thought that if it were left alone it would simply follow the huia into oblivion, and wanted birds moved to island sanctuaries and bred in captivity. With little money and no agreement, almost nothing was done for nearly a decade.

The Murchisons, meanwhile, were filling up with red deer. Takahē live largely on the leaves of snow tussock and other alpine grasses, and so, it turned out, do deer. With deer competing for the same plants, a population that had numbered around 400 was down to 118 by 1982.

The recovery that followed is a catalogue of patient, unglamorous technique. Deer were shot from helicopters until the tussock came back. From 1984, birds were flown to predator-free islands: Maud Island in the Marlborough Sounds, then Mana, Kāpiti, Tiritiri Matangi and Motutapu. In 1985 a breeding centre opened at Burwood, near Te Anau, where eggs were incubated and chicks hand-reared by staff who fed them with takahē-headed puppets and played them recordings of adult calls, so that the birds wouldn't grow up thinking they were people. Infertile eggs were removed from wild nests. Birds were counted, weighed and fitted with transmitters.

It has worked, slowly. The takahē population was 263 in 2013, 347 in 2017, 418 in 2019. In 2018 eighteen birds were released into Kahurangi National Park, in the north-west of the South Island, a century after takahē had vanished from there. In 2023 another eighteen went into the Greenstone Valley, on Ngāi Tahu land near the head of Lake Whakatipu, and further releases followed. Not all of them have gone well; stoats killed four of the Greenstone birds in a single five-week stretch in 2025. But by the Department of Conservation's 2024 tally there were an estimated 529 takahē alive, the most since anyone began counting.

There are now so many takahē, in so many places, with so few of them still wearing transmitters, that the Department has said a single population figure has become "more difficult, and less meaningful" to report. From now on it will give a range. For a species once known from four dead birds, that is a very good problem to have.

## What a footprint is worth

The takahē has become the patron saint of hope. It is invoked every time someone reports a moa in the Fiordland bush or a thylacine in a Tasmanian headlight: *the takahē was gone for fifty years, and look*. That argument deserves some care. Orbell didn't find the takahē because he believed in it. He found it because he had a specific, testable idea about where it would be, because the country was genuinely hard to search, because the bird was small enough to hide there, and because, in the end, it left footprints. Fifty years of sightings had produced nothing. One morning of tracks produced a species.

What the takahē really teaches is less romantic and more useful. Absence of evidence in difficult country is weak evidence. The people most likely to know are the ones who are actually out there: hunters, trampers, the iwi who had always known the bird. And a rediscovery is not an ending. It is the start of a long, expensive, dull and magnificent piece of work.

Geoffrey Orbell died in 2007, at the age of 98. By then the takahē had stopped being a miracle and become a programme, with budgets and spreadsheets and annual reports. Every takahē in the country now shares an official hatch-day, the first of October, so that the Department can keep its books in order. That, more than the morning in the valley, is the happy ending.

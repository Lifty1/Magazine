standfirst: Nobody is going to rediscover a moa. So a Texan company has offered to make one. Rediscovery and resurrection sound like the same miracle. They are not, and the difference matters.

There is no animal New Zealanders would rather find alive than a moa. There were nine species. The largest, the two species of *Dinornis*, could reach around 3.6 metres tall with the neck stretched up, and weighed about as much as a big stag. They were the only birds known to have lost their wings completely, not even a stub of bone remaining. Their closest living relatives, it turns out, are the tinamous of South America, small, dumpy birds that can still fly.

Polynesian voyagers reached Aotearoa some time before 1300. Within a century or so, according to radiocarbon dates from oven sites and middens, every moa was dead. By about 1445 they were gone, and so was Haast's eagle, the enormous raptor that had hunted them. It was one of the fastest extinctions of large animals ever recorded, and the moa have been haunting the country ever since.

They have also been sighted. Nineteenth-century surveyors reported giant footprints; whalers and sealers told stories of monstrous birds on the coast. In 1959 an elderly woman named Alice McKenzie said she had seen a moa in Fiordland as a girl in the 1880s. In 1993 three people walking in the Craigieburn Range produced a blurred photograph of a large animal that interested the Department of Conservation for a while; it was generally judged to be a red deer.

People who want to believe sometimes invoke the takahē: gone for fifty years, and found. It is a fair point about the limits of searching, and a poor one about moa. The takahē is the size of a large chicken and left footprints that a determined man could follow. A moa is the size of a small horse. To still be out there it would have to have lived unnoticed for five centuries in country crossed every week by hunters, trampers and deer-cullers, without leaving one convincing track, feather, dropping or bone. The moa is not missing. It is extinct. It will not be found.

## The offer

On 8 July 2025, a company called Colossal Biosciences announced that it was going to bring it back anyway.

Colossal, based in Dallas, is the best known of the de-extinction companies, valued after its January 2025 funding round at more than ten billion US dollars. It has said it intends to produce woolly mammoth-like elephants, a thylacine, a dodo. Its moa project is a partnership with the Ngāi Tahu Research Centre at the University of Canterbury, which is described as leading it, and with Canterbury Museum, whose collection of moa bones supplies the raw material. The film-maker Sir Peter Jackson, a moa enthusiast, and his partner Fran Walsh have put US$15 million into it.

The plan, as Colossal has described it, goes roughly like this. Sequence the genomes of all nine moa species from DNA in bones. Work out which genes made a moa a moa, by comparing them with its living relatives; the emu and the tinamous are the leading candidates. Edit those changes into the cells of a living bird. Then turn the edited cells into an animal.

That last step is harder for birds than for mammals. A bird can't be cloned the way Dolly the sheep was, because a bird's egg can't be removed, emptied and refilled with a new nucleus. The likely route runs instead through germ cells, the precursors of sperm and eggs, edited and introduced into a host bird so that it lays eggs carrying the new genes. And no living bird lays an egg big enough for a giant moa. In May 2026 Colossal announced what it called a major breakthrough: an artificial egg, a transparent shell made of a silicone-based lattice that lets oxygen in, from which it had hatched twenty-six chickens. "There's currently no surrogate bird species that lays an egg big enough," its chief biology officer, Andrew Pask, told RNZ, "so we had to make the Colossal artificial egg."

## What would hatch

It is worth being precise about what this could produce, because Colossal has already shown us.

In April 2025 the company announced the birth of three "dire wolves", Romulus, Remus and Khaleesi, white, heavy-set pups that appeared on magazine covers around the world. Dire wolves died out more than ten thousand years ago. The pups were grey wolves whose genomes had been edited in about twenty places to give them some of the dire wolf's traits. The IUCN's specialist group on wild dogs said flatly that they were neither dire wolves nor proxies of dire wolves. A month later Colossal's own chief scientist, Beth Shapiro, described them as "grey wolves with 20 edits", and said that bringing back an animal identical to one that used to be alive is not possible.

A Colossal moa, then, would be a large flightless bird, probably built from an emu, with some moa genes, hatched from a plastic egg and raised by people. It would have no moa parents to learn from, and nothing to learn about: much of the forest it browsed has been felled or changed beyond recognition, the plants it ate have spent five hundred years evolving without it, and the eagle that hunted it is gone. The University of Otago ancient-DNA specialist Nic Rawlence, one of the project's most outspoken critics, has called it a genetically modified emu. Another Otago zoologist, Philip Seddon, said when it was announced that it had "nothing much to do with solving the global extinction crisis and more to do with generating fundraising media coverage". Colossal rejected that.

## The case for

The project's supporters have real arguments, and they deserve a hearing.

Ancient genomes are scientifically valuable whatever happens next. They can tell us how many moa there were, how their populations rose and fell, how they were related, and what Aotearoa was like before people arrived. Tools developed for an impossible goal can turn out to be useful for possible ones. An artificial egg that lets you watch a chick develop, Pask argues, opens up new ways to help bird species that are struggling now. The money, too, is not being taken from the Department of Conservation; it is private money that would mostly never have gone to conservation at all. And the project, its partners say, is being steered by Māori, for whom moa are taonga, not by a lab in Texas.

## The case against

The strongest objection is not that it can't be done. It is what the doing teaches us.

Every rediscovery story in this magazine has the same moral. The takahē, the cahow, the tree lobster, the tāiko: each was found alive because enough of it had survived, somewhere, by itself, carrying not only its genes but its knowledge, how to find food, how to choose a mate, where to dig a burrow, when to come home. Each was then saved, slowly, by people who changed the place it lived in: shot the deer, poisoned the rats, built the fences, planted the trees. None was saved by a machine.

De-extinction offers a seductive alternative story: that extinction is a technical problem, and that a sufficiently well-funded laboratory can reverse it. If that idea takes hold, it lowers the cost of losing things. Why spend decades and millions keeping the last two hundred tāiko alive on one hillside, if we can always print a new one later? Colossal says it believes the opposite, that its technology is meant to stop extinctions as well as reverse them. But the stories a culture tells about extinction shape what it is willing to pay to prevent it.

And there is a question nobody can answer in a laboratory, which is who gets to decide. Moa were hunted by the ancestors of iwi throughout both islands. A taonga is not just an animal; it carries obligations. Colossal's position is that consultation is a matter for Māori to judge. That is respectful, and it is also, for a company with a ten-billion-dollar valuation, convenient.

## Lazarus walked out

The magazine you are holding is named after a man who, in the story, was dead and buried for four days before he was called back. It is worth remembering the detail that gives the story its force: Lazarus walked out of the tomb himself. He was not assembled.

That is the difference between the takahē and anything that will hatch from an artificial egg in Canterbury. A rediscovered species is a gift: a population that kept itself going, against the odds, until we noticed. A resurrected one would be a product. It might be an extraordinary product, and the science behind it might do real good. But it would not be a moa, and it should not let anyone believe that losing something is reversible.

The cheapest way to have a moa was never to lose it. We did, six hundred years ago. We still have kākāpō, takahē and tāiko. That is where the money should go first.

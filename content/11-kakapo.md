---
kicker: Aotearoa · 1977
title: Nearly
standfirst: The kākāpō was never declared extinct. It came closer than any bird that survived.
---

There is no other bird like the kākāpō: a moss-green, owl-faced, flightless parrot that weighs as much as a small cat, walks everywhere, climbs trees with its beak and feet, smells faintly of honey, and breeds mainly in the years when the rimu trees fruit heavily. In those years the males dig bowls into hillsides and spend whole nights inflating themselves like bagpipes to make a deep *boom* that carries for kilometres, so that females will walk across the island to inspect them.

All of this worked well for millions of years in a country with no land mammals but bats. Its defence against the eagles and harriers that hunted by daylight was to come out at night and, if threatened, freeze, trusting its camouflage. Against dogs, cats, rats and stoats, which hunt by smell, freezing is useless. By the early 1970s nobody could be sure the kākāpō still existed.

The Wildlife Service had been searching Fiordland since the 1950s. Between 1974 and 1978 it found eighteen birds there, in steep glacial valleys. All of them were males. A species made entirely of males is a species with no future, and for a while it seemed that was what the kākāpō had become.

Then, in 1977, kākāpō were reported on Stewart Island/Rakiura, where nobody had thought to look. There were perhaps one or two hundred of them, and among them, crucially, were females. It was not a rediscovery of the species, quite, but it was a rediscovery of its future.

It was nearly lost again at once. Feral cats on Stewart Island were killing more than half the birds each year. Between 1982 and 1997 every kākāpō that could be caught was moved to predator-free islands: Little Barrier, Maud, Codfish Island/Whenua Hou, and later Anchor. In 1995, when the Kākāpō Recovery Programme was set up, there were 51 birds left in the world.

What followed is perhaps the most intensive care any wild species has ever received. Every kākāpō has a name. Every one wears a transmitter. Their food is supplemented, their nests watched by camera, their eggs incubated when necessary and their chicks hand-raised when needed. Their sperm has been collected and artificial insemination attempted, and their genomes sequenced, every one, so that matings can be planned to protect what little genetic diversity is left.

It has been slow, and it has not always been happy: disease outbreaks, failed seasons, years without rimu fruit. But the numbers have gone the right way. By the latest count, in 2026, there were 325 kākāpō.

They are not safe. They may never be safe. Their entire world consists of a handful of offshore islands, and they will need people to look after them for as long as anyone can foresee. But almost every one of those 325 birds is alive because, in 1977, someone went somewhere nobody had bothered to look.

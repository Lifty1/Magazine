Seabirds are the easiest animals in the world to lose. They spend most of their lives far from land, they come ashore only to breed, often on remote islands, and many of the smaller petrels arrive and leave their burrows only in darkness. A species can be reduced to a few dozen pairs in a single steep gully and still pass, for decades, entirely unnoticed by people a few kilometres away. Both of these birds did exactly that.

## I. The tāiko

On 22 July 1867 the Italian naval ship *Magenta*, crossing the South Pacific roughly midway between New Zealand and South America, collected a dark-backed, white-bellied petrel that no naturalist had seen before. It was named for the ship, *Pterodroma magentae*, the Magenta petrel, and for 111 years the specimen in Turin was the only one known.

It was not, in fact, a stranger to New Zealand. On Rēkohu, the main Chatham Island, eight hundred kilometres east of Christchurch, bones in middens showed that the tāiko had once been the most abundant burrowing seabird on the island and an important food for Moriori, the islands' first people. Pigs, cats, rats and the clearing of the forest had all but wiped it out. Nobody could be sure that the bones on the Chathams and the skin in Italy belonged to the same bird.

The ornithologist David Crockett, from Whangārei, spent years trying to find out. Through the 1970s he led a series of expeditions to the south-west of Chatham Island, following up local reports of a strange bird. The method was hard, cold and nocturnal: standing on the hills in the dark with powerful spotlights, waiting for petrels to fly into the beam. On 1 January 1978, in the Tuku valley, he caught a tāiko.

Finding the bird was one thing. Finding where it nested took another decade, and radio transmitters fitted to birds caught in the spotlights. The burrows, when they were found, were long tunnels dug under dense forest on land farmed by the Tuanui family. In 1983 Manuel and Evelyn Tuanui gave more than a thousand hectares of it to the Crown to protect the birds. It is now the Tuku Nature Reserve.

The tāiko remains one of the rarest seabirds on Earth: fewer than 200 birds, with only around twenty breeding burrows known. Since 2006 a predator-proof fence has enclosed a hilltop at Sweetwater, and chicks have been moved there by hand so that they will return to breed somewhere cats and rats can't reach. Crockett died in 2023. The bird he chased through the dark for a decade is still here.

## II. The storm petrel

The New Zealand storm petrel is a tiny thing, about the size of a sparrow, dark above with a white rump and a streaked white belly, and it feeds by pattering across the sea surface on long legs, wings raised, as if walking on water. In the nineteenth century a handful of specimens were collected in New Zealand waters and ended up in museums in England and France. After about 1850 there were no more. For a century and a half the species was known from those few skins, and was listed as extinct.

On 25 January 2003 two birders, Sav Saville and Brent Stephenson, were on a boat near the Mercury Islands off the Coromandel Peninsula when a small storm petrel came pattering past that didn't look like anything it should have been. Their photographs were inconclusive. On 17 November that year, out in the Hauraki Gulf looking for other storm petrels, Bob Flood and Bryan Thomas photographed and filmed between ten and twenty of them off Little Barrier and Great Barrier islands. There was no longer any doubt. A bird thought extinct for 150 years was feeding within sight of Auckland, in one of the busiest stretches of recreational water in the country.

What nobody knew was where it bred. Over the next few years researchers caught birds at sea, fitted three with radio transmitters, and followed them. It took until February 2013 for a team led by Chris Gaskin and Matt Rayner to track the birds to their burrows: in the forest on Te Hauturu-o-Toi, Little Barrier Island. A year later the first New Zealand storm petrel egg was found, white with a fine dusting of pink at one end, 31 millimetres long.

Hauturu has been one of New Zealand's most important island sanctuaries for more than a century, and its introduced cats and Pacific rats have since been eradicated. One plausible explanation for the storm petrel's reappearance is that a remnant population had hung on there all along, too small to notice, and began to recover once the island's predators were gone. Nobody can know for certain. What is certain is that the birds are back on the water every summer, and that boatloads of birders now go out of Auckland to watch them walk on it.
